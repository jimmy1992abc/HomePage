---
title: "A reconciliation is an argument, not a checklist"
description: "Ticking the box proves the task was done. It does not prove the balance is right, and that proof is the reason the task exists."

Most close checklists have a line that says "bank reconciliation completed" with a tick beside it. The tick records that somebody did something. It says nothing about whether the balance is right.

I think of a reconciliation as an argument. The claim is that this balance in the ledger is correct. The reconciliation is the evidence for that claim, set out so that someone who was not there can follow it and agree. A reconciliation that can't persuade a sceptical reader hasn't done its job, however neatly it is formatted.

## What the argument needs

**An independent source.** The ledger can't be evidence for itself. A bank statement, a supplier statement, a subledger maintained by a different process, a count of what is physically there. If both sides come from the same place, the reconciliation proves nothing.

**Every difference named.** A reconciling item is a small claim of its own: this is the difference, this is why it exists, and this is when it will clear. "Timing" is not an explanation. A payment in transit that was presented two days later is.

**Age.** Reconciling items should be born and die within a period or two. An item that has been carried for six months has stopped being a timing difference. It is now an error that nobody has owned. The most useful column on any reconciliation is how long each item has been there.

**A conclusion.** The last line should say, in words, that the balance is supported, or that it is not and by how much. A schedule that ends in a difference of zero and no sentence leaves the reader to decide what it means.

## Where it goes wrong

The usual failure is the plug. The two sides don't agree, the deadline is close, and a line appears called "unreconciled difference" or "other". The schedule now ties. The argument has collapsed, because the one thing the reconciliation was supposed to explain is the thing it has declined to explain.

The second failure is the roll-forward. Last month's reconciliation is copied, the dates are changed, and the same items are carried again. The schedule ties every month and gets a little more out of date each time.

Both are checklist thinking. The task was to produce a schedule that ties, and it was done.

## Why this matters more now

Software can match transactions faster than any person, and a model can draft the explanations. That makes the checklist version of a reconciliation nearly free. It makes the argument more valuable, because a tool will happily produce a schedule that ties and reads well.

So the review question has to be the old one. Would I be persuaded by this if I had never seen the account before? Is the other side really independent? Is each item explained, and is anything getting old? If I can't say yes to those, the tick shouldn't go on the checklist, whoever or whatever prepared the schedule.
