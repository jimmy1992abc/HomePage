There is a question I ask about every piece of finance work before I let a model near it: when this number turns out to be wrong, who explains it?

If the answer is "the model", the design is broken. A model cannot attend the meeting. It cannot be asked what it was thinking in March. It will not remember, and it does not carry the consequences. Accountability needs a person, and that part of the job has not moved an inch in the last three years, even as the cost of preparing the work has collapsed.

That gap is the most useful thing to understand about AI in finance. Preparation and ownership used to be bundled, because the person who built the schedule was usually the person who understood it. They are now coming apart.

## Preparing and owning are different jobs

A model is a very good preparer. It reads every line of a contract. It doesn't get bored on the four-hundredth transaction. It will draft an accrual, write the narrative that goes with it, and format the working paper, in less time than it takes to open the file.

What it can't do is own the result. Owning a number means three things:

- You know where it came from, down to the source document.
- You know which judgements are inside it and could defend each one.
- You would notice if it were wrong.

The third is the one that gets lost. A preparer who built a schedule by hand develops a feel for it. They know the balance is usually around a certain size and moves for certain reasons. When the model builds the schedule, nobody acquires that feel unless the process is designed to give it to them.

## Review has to get harder, not easier

The tempting conclusion is that if the model prepares, the human reviews, and review is quicker than preparation, so everyone goes home early.

It doesn't work that way. Reviewing work you did not prepare, produced by something fluent and confident, is harder than reviewing a junior's work. A junior's mistakes look like mistakes: a formula dragged one row too far, a total that doesn't tie. A model's mistakes look like finished work. The narrative reads well. The format is right. The number is plausible and wrong.

So the review has to be built on things that do not depend on how convincing the output looks:

- **Tie-outs.** The schedule agrees to the ledger, and a script checks it, not a sentence saying it does.
- **Source links.** Every figure points at the document it came from, so a reviewer can pull three at random and look.
- **Movement analysis.** What changed since last period and why, because an unexplained movement is where errors live.
- **A stated judgement list.** The model writes down every assumption it made. The reviewer reads that list first.

None of this is new. It is what a good working paper has always contained. The difference is that it used to be optional polish and is now the only thing standing between a fluent draft and a wrong number.

## A simple rule

Here is the rule I use. The model may do anything that I can check faster than I could do. It may not do anything where my only way of checking is to trust it.

Matching a thousand bank lines passes that test, because the unmatched residue is small and I can read it. Drafting variance commentary passes, because I know the business and can tell in a minute whether the explanation is true. Deciding whether a contract contains a lease does not pass on its own. I can use the model to find the clauses and lay out the argument, and then the judgement is mine, written down, with my name on it.

The profession is not going to be replaced by the thing that prepares the journal. It may well be reshaped around the person who can still explain it.
